How to ask for a raise: what actually works

When is the best time to ask for a raise?

Knowing when to ask for a raise matters as much as knowing what to say. The best time is when three things align: your company has budget authority (typically before fiscal year-end or during review cycles), your manager has political capital (they're not under fire or dealing with layoffs), and you have a recent, visible win. Waiting for the "perfect moment" is a mistake — but walking in during a round of cost-cutting is worse. The most successful raise conversations happen 2-4 weeks before a scheduled performance review, when your boss is already thinking about compensation and you have time to prepare your raise plan properly.

What to say when asking for a raise

The biggest mistake people make is opening with "I'd like to discuss my compensation." That sentence puts your manager on the defensive immediately. Instead, open by framing the conversation around your contributions and the value you bring forward — not backward. Something like "I'd like to talk about my role and where it's heading" shifts the dynamic from a request to a strategic conversation. Your boss becomes an ally helping you plan your growth, not a gatekeeper protecting the budget. The right way to ask for a raise is to lead with value delivered, not money wanted. If you're underpaid, let the data make the case — not the emotion. Not sure what to say when asking for a raise? That's exactly what the scenario planning tool helps you figure out — you see the exact words for every situation.

How much of a raise should you ask for?

If you're wondering how much should I ask for a raise, you're already ahead of most people. Most employees ask for "more" without naming a number — and that's the single most common reason pay raise negotiations fail. A vague ask gives your manager permission to offer the minimum. Research shows that anchoring with a specific, slightly ambitious number leads to better outcomes than giving a range. If you say "I'm looking for between 8% and 12%," your manager hears "8%." If you say "I'm looking for 12% based on my market research," the negotiation starts there. The right number should be high enough to matter to you, specific enough to be taken seriously, and defensible enough that you can explain why. Knowing your raise probability before you walk in helps you calibrate how ambitious to be.

What to do if your raise request is denied

If your boss said no to a raise, you're not alone — and it's not over. A "no" is almost never a permanent no. It's usually "not right now," "I need to justify it to my boss," or "I don't have the budget this quarter." The key is knowing what to say next. Ask specifically: "What would need to change for this to be a yes?" and "Can we revisit this in [specific timeframe]?" Then follow up with an email summarizing the conversation and the agreed timeline. If you asked for a raise and got nothing, the worst thing you can do is accept it silently. Most people do — which is exactly why they stay underpaid at work. The people who successfully get a raise treat the first conversation as the beginning of a process, not a one-shot attempt. If you haven't had a raise in years, that's not normal — it's a signal that you need to start the conversation, not wait for someone to notice.

How to negotiate a salary increase without sounding pushy

The fear of seeming greedy or pushy stops more people from asking for a raise than any other single factor. But here's what experienced negotiators know: the best raise conversations don't feel like negotiations at all. They feel like two people solving a problem together. Your boss doesn't want to lose you — replacing an employee costs 50-200% of their annual salary. When you negotiate a raise by framing your ask as "I want to make sure my compensation reflects the role I'm actually doing," you're not being pushy. You're being professional. The pushiest thing you can do is stay silent, grow resentful, and eventually leave — which costs everyone more. Having a raise plan with prepared responses for every scenario is the right way to negotiate salary without the anxiety.

How to ask for a raise at work: step by step

Step one: document your contributions over the past 6-12 months — specific results, not job duties. Step two: research what your role pays in your market using multiple sources. Step three: decide on a specific number and write down your opening sentence. Step four: schedule a private meeting with your manager — don't ambush them. Step five: lead with your contributions, present your number, and be prepared for pushback. Step six: whatever the outcome, send a follow-up email summarizing the conversation. The entire process takes a few hours of preparation for a conversation that can change your earnings for years. That's how you get a raise — not by hoping, but by preparing for every scenario your boss might throw at you.
