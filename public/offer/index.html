Will countering put my offer at risk?+
Rescinding an offer over a professional, respectful counter is extremely rare. Companies expect candidates to negotiate — most build room into the first number for exactly that reason. The risk comes from countering badly: ultimatums, bluffing, or numbers with no reasoning. The kit is designed so your counter signals enthusiasm to sign, not a threat to walk.
Where do the numbers come from?+
From negotiation mechanics, not invented market data. Companies systematically anchor first offers below their approved band ceiling. Why most people accept the first number — and what it costs them

Most candidates accept a job offer without negotiating at all. Not because the offer was their ceiling — companies systematically anchor first offers 5–15% below the band they already approved — but because the conversation feels risky. The fear is always the same: what if they rescind? In reality, pulling an offer over a professional counter is extremely rare. The real risk is the silent one: a starting salary that's thousands low and compounds for every year and every future job that anchors on it.

How to write a counter offer email — one number, one signal

A good counter offer email is short. Thank them genuinely. Name one number for base — base compounds forever, so it leads. Attach your reasoning in one sentence. Then give the signal recruiters actually want: "if we can get there, I'm ready to sign this week." That line converts your counter from a risk into a closing opportunity. No ultimatums, no bluffing, no apologizing for asking.

How to handle "this is our best and final offer"

"Best and final" on the first mention is usually a negotiation move, not a fact. The professional response doesn't fight the base — it pivots: a signing bonus is one-time budget that doesn't touch their salary band, which makes it the easiest yes in the building. Knowing the pivot is half the job; having practiced saying it out loud is the other half.

How to negotiate a job offer without losing it

The counters that put offers at risk share a pattern: ultimatums, invented competing offers, numbers far outside any defensible range, or a tone that signals you'd rather win than join. The counters that work share the opposite pattern: enthusiasm first, one clear ask, reasoning attached, and a visible path to yes. Negotiating a job offer without losing it isn't about being timid — it's about making your counter feel like the last step before you sign.

How do I negotiate my first job offer?

If this is your first real offer, the instinct is to be grateful and sign fast. Negotiate anyway — your first base salary is the anchor every future raise and offer builds on. You don't need experience; you need a plan and one rehearsal. Practice the call with an AI coach that plays the recruiter — the pushback, the deadline pressure, the "what were you expecting?" — until your answers come out steady. The first time you say your number out loud should not be on the real call.
