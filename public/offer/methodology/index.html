The five signals

What actually moves your number

Every one of these comes directly from an answer you give, and each maps to a specific, named concept in negotiation and pricing theory — nothing here is inferred from vibes.

01
Walk-Away Power
Negotiation theory calls this your BATNA — Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement. It's the single largest predictor of outcome in any negotiation: how much you personally lose if this specific offer falls through, governed by your employment status and whether you have competing offers already in motion.
Example: a competing offer in hand raises your BATNA meaningfully above being between jobs with none — the room above your ask exists whether or not you use it, but a real alternative is what lets you actually ask.
02
Risk Tolerance
How you described your own approach — safe, balanced, or ambitious. This doesn't change the room that exists; it changes where on the expected-value curve we recommend you actually ask, trading acceptance probability against upside the same way any rational offer would.
03
Their Urgency
Economists call this a costly signal — an action a company wouldn't bother taking if it weren't genuinely interested. How fast they moved and whether they followed up unprompted cost them real time and attention; a tight deadline costs them nothing, so it's scored as pressure, not as a signal of interest.
Example: two unprompted follow-ups is a costly signal and scores as real urgency. A 48-hour deadline is free to write into an offer letter and is weighed separately, as pressure rather than eagerness.
04
Talent Market
The economics term is elasticity — how sensitive a company's decision is to your specific price. How replaceable you are in this role, driven by your years of experience and how common your work type is at this level, determines how elastic their position actually is.
05
Offer Structure
Governed by anchoring bias — the well-documented tendency for the first number on the table to gravitationally pull the final one. Where the money already sits relative to what you asked for, and how rigid compensation bands tend to be at a company this size, sets how much that anchor can still move.
From five scores to one number

How the range gets built

A composite score, not a single formula pulled from thin air — closer to how a compensation committee reasons through a counter than to a lookup table.

1
Each of the five signals is scored from your specific answers against its underlying theory — BATNA, signaling cost, elasticity, anchoring, expected value — not a language model guessing. The same method, applied the same way, every time.
2
The five scores combine into a leverage position — a single composite read of how much room genuinely exists above your base offer, weighted so no one signal can dominate the read on its own.
3
That position sets your range, then splits into three real asks — Safe, Strong, and Bold — each with its own likelihood of being accepted.
4
Only after the number exists does AI get involved — to write the email and pushback responses around a number that was already decided by the method, not by it.

What's calculated, and what's written

We're not going to pretend AI has no role here — it does, and pretending otherwise wouldn't hold up. Here's exactly where the line sits.

Calculated, not generated: your range, your three tiers, your risk score, your acceptance percentages. These come from the Offer-Structure Method's scoring — the same method, applied the same way, every time.

AI-assisted: the wording of your counter email and pushback responses. The number they're built around was already decided before any writing happens.
